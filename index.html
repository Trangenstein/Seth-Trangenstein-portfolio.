What it covers

The defense industrial base isn't a chain, it's a pyramid: five primes (Lockheed Martin, RTX, Northrop Grumman, General Dynamics, Boeing) sit on top of thin-margin, thinly-capitalized tier-2 and tier-3 suppliers the government has almost no visibility into. Below that, the entire base runs through a handful of chokepoints — rare-earth and critical-mineral processing dominated by China, and a semiconductor supply chain built on mature process nodes rather than leading-edge silicon.

The second half tracks the federal response: Defense Production Act Title III funding, DoD's equity stake in MP Materials, and the CHIPS-funded Microelectronics Commons — set against a 2027 deadline to phase out Chinese-origin material, and analysts' warnings that the dependency won't unwind with a single investment.

What it covers

Traces the "commercial item" loophole, sole-source leverage, and cost-plus incentive structures that let contractors set prices with no market to prove them against — walked through four case studies: TransDigm's acquire-then-raise-prices playbook, an 80x markup on Boeing C-17 spare parts, redacted overcharges at Bell Helicopter, and F-35 sustainment costs climbing as readiness falls.

Closes with the 2025–26 legislative response: five bipartisan bills that require contractors to disclose price jumps after the fact, folded into an NDAA that also repeals roughly 80 procurement regulations — a disclosure-not-prevention tradeoff the piece leaves as an open question.
